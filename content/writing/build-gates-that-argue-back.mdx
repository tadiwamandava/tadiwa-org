I spent the first day of this site's build writing about as much tooling as
interface. That felt indulgent at the time. By the end of the day the tooling had
caught three defects I had already looked at directly and approved.

That is the whole argument for build gates, so here are the three.

## 1. A border I thought was fine was at 1.88:1

I picked the palette by eye. Warm cream, warm near-black, a rust accent. It
looked good, and I have picked enough palettes to trust that feeling.

Then I wrote a script that parses the token blocks straight out of the stylesheet
and computes WCAG contrast for every foreground/background pair the design
actually uses. Forty pairs, both themes.

Three failed.

Two were tertiary text on a card, 4.14:1 against a 4.5:1 requirement. Close
enough that no amount of squinting would have caught it, far enough that it
genuinely fails for a lot of people.

The third was worse:

```
✗ emphasised border    1.88:1 (min 3)   --border-strong on --bg
```

`--border-strong` is the token for borders that identify a control, the outline
of a text input. WCAG requires 3:1 for that, because a border you cannot see is a
form field you cannot find. I had it at 1.88. On the page it read as a
tasteful hairline. That is exactly the problem: to me it was tasteful, and to
someone else it was absent.

The fix was ten minutes. Noticing was the hard part, and I did not notice. A
script did.

One detail that turned out to matter: **the script parses the real stylesheet
rather than keeping its own copy of the palette.** The tempting version hardcodes
the hex values in the checker. That version drifts the first time you tweak a
colour, and from then on it certifies a theme nobody is shipping. A gate that
can silently pass the wrong thing is worse than no gate.

## 2. Unlayered CSS beats every cascade layer

I wrote base element styles the way I always have:

```css
h1, h2, h3, h4 {
  font-family: var(--font-fraunces), Georgia, serif;
}
```

Then I built a section heading with a `font-mono` utility class on an `<h2>`, and
it rendered in the serif.

Tailwind v4 puts its utilities inside `@layer utilities`. My base styles were
unlayered. And unlayered CSS does not lose to a layer. It wins against **all** of
them, regardless of specificity. So a bare `h2` element selector was beating
`.font-mono`, and every font utility on every heading across the site was silently
dead.

I did not find this by reasoning about the cascade. I found it because I took a
screenshot, looked at it, and the words `SELECTED WORK` were in the wrong
typeface.

Which is its own lesson. I had a build passing, a typecheck passing, and a
contrast gate passing, and the page was still wrong in a way that only eyes catch.
Automated gates catch the class of error you can specify. Looking catches the
rest, and neither substitutes for the other.

## 3. Two colours I thought were distinct measured ΔE 11.7

The benchmark charts compare my implementation against reference implementations:
my key-value store against Redis, my `grep` against ripgrep. Two kinds of bar, so
two colours.

I picked rust `#9c3d17` for mine and a warm grey-brown `#6b6151` for the
references. They looked different to me.

Running them through a perceptual-distance check:

```
[FAIL] Normal-vision floor   #6b6151 ↔ #9c3d17   ΔE 11.7, below 15,
                             hard to tell apart even with full colour vision
[WARN] CVD separation        ΔE 6.8 (deutan)
```

Not "hard for colourblind readers". Hard for *everyone*, and then considerably
worse for the roughly one in twelve men with a colour-vision deficiency. Both of
my colours were mid-tone warm browns. Of course they were: they came out of the
same warm palette, and my eye had already normalised to it.

The fix was not a better guess. It was realising the two bars are not peers at
all. My result is the subject; the reference is context. Once framed that way the
answer is obvious. The reference bar should be visibly recessive, well separated
in lightness, not a competing peer colour. The replacement measures ΔE 27.8.

## The pattern

All three were things I had looked directly at and approved. Not things I
skipped, but things I inspected and got wrong, because eyes normalise, and taste is not a
measurement.

The gates that now fail this build:

- **contrast**: every token pair, both themes, and a parity check that the
  media-query dark theme and the toggle-selected dark theme are byte-identical
- **content**: every article validated against a schema before it can render
- **types**, **lint**, **build**

None of them is clever. All of them are faster than the bug they prevent.

The corollary I keep having to relearn: a gate is only worth having if it can fail.
A checker that reads a copy of the data it is checking, or a threshold quietly
lowered until things pass, is a green checkmark that means nothing. A green
checkmark that means nothing is more dangerous than a red one, because you stop
looking.
